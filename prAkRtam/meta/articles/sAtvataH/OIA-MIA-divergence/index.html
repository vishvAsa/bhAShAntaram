Vedic - Classical closeness

I fully agree that the Sanskrit of RV is indeed more archaic than standard Sanskrit, but the point highlighted here is that Vedic Sanskrit is just barely a distinct language from Classical Sanskrit and both languages ultimately belong to the same grammatical and typological category (i.e. Old Indo-Aryan).

Prakrit distinctness

In contrast, Prakrits are more distinct and belong to a separate class known as Middle Indo-Aryan.

“In Middle Indo-Aryan, the dual number has collapsed,
the dative has largely merged with the genitive,
there is a tendency to merge the optative mood w/ the imperative mood (particularly in later MIA),
intervocalic stops undergo voicing→ fricativization→ elision,
consonant clusters are typically replaced by geminates,
the past tense system has largely been reworked (i.e. the perfect tense disappears relatively early in MIA
and although aorist and imperfect survive a little longer,
nearly all finite past forms are replaced by past participial constructions in later MIA),
and the declensional system has been simplified (albeit with far greater dialectical variation).”

dvivacana

Source: TW

Sanskrit/OIA uses the dual number (dvivacana) to express a dual agent, referent, etc.

To use an illustration from Patañjali’s Mahābhāṣyam, it would be unidiomatic for one to say:

“akṣīṇi me darśanīyāni, pādā me sukumārāḥ” (My eyes are beautiful and my feet are very delicate)

The use of the plural here suggests that the speaker has three or more eyes and three or more feet, which is not the case.

In this context, the speaker should have instead employed the dual, i.e. “akṣiṇī me darśanīye, pādau me sukumārau”

In contrast to Sanskrit, Prakrit has only ekavacana and anekavacana/bahuvacana.

It lacks a “dvivacana” because the plural covers the dual as well, which is why in the Rāvaṇavaho, Rāma tells Nala (i.e. the vānara son of Viśvakarman, not the Naiṣāda king and paramour of Damayantī):

taṃ bandhasu, dhīra!, tumaṃ seuṃ ajjea jāva dūrantariā। ekkaṃ Malaʸa-Suvelā hontu duhā ʸa viaḍā samuddaddhantā॥

tato badhāna, dhīra!, tvaṃ setum adyaiva yāvad dūrāntaritau। ekaṃ Malaya-Suvelau bhavatāṃ dvidhā/dvedhā/dvaidhaṃ ca vikaṭau samudrārdhāntau॥

“Therefore, O steadfast one!, build a bridge that stretches so far that the widely-separated Malaya and Suvela [mountains] become [connected together as] one and the ocean is bisected into two vast halves.”

Due to the absence of dual number in Prakrit, dūrantariā (dūrāntaritāḥ) is used for dūrāntaritau, Malaʸa-Suvelā (Malaya-Suvelāḥ) for Malaya-Suvelau, hontu (bhavantu) for bhavatām, viaḍā for vikaṭāḥ (vikaṭau), and samuddaddhantā (samudrārdhāntāḥ) for samudrārdhāntau.

Although the poem Rāvaṇavaho (= Rāvaṇavadhaḥ, conventionally referred to as Setubandham) is traditionally attributed to the Vākāṭaka king Pravarasena II, it is said to have been revised by Kālidāsa, who was a contemporary of Pravarasena II.

Some have even argued that Pravarasena II is the “Kuntaleśvara” of Kālidāsa’s Kuntaleśvaradautya, which is now lost (known only via Kṣemendra’s Aucityavicāracarcā and Bhoja’s Śṛṅgāraprakāśa).

ṣaṣṭhī as caturthī

In Prakrit, the genitive (ṣaṣṭhī) takes over most functions of the Sanskrit dative (caturthī).

As an example:

chittammi jeṇa ramiā tāo kira tassa maṃ dei। jaï teṇa imaṃ nisuaṃ phuṭṭaï hiaʸaṃ harisiāe॥

[kṣetre yena ramitā tātaḥ kila tasmā eva māṃ dadāti। yadi tenedaṃ niśrutaṃ, sphuṭati hṛdayaṃ harṣikayā॥]

My father is giving me in marriage precisely to him with whom I sported in the field. If he [i.e. my lover] has heard this, [my] heart bursts with joy.

  • Gāhāsattasaī

Here tassa (= tasya) is used where one would use tasmai in Sanskrit (cf. karmaṇā yam abhipraiti sa sampradānam, caturthī sampradāne)

While most functions of the Sanskrit dative have merged with the morphological genitive in Prakrit, the tādarthye caturthī of Sanskrit is unique insofar as it still survives in Prakrit, though the retention of the dative affix is optional and applies only to the singular affix -Ṅe (tādarthya-ṅer vā).

The word vadha- (vaha- in Prakrit) may also take the affix -Ḍāi (cf. vadhāḍ ḍāiś ca vā).
This results in three ways to express the tādarthya sense with the word “vadha-” (slaying) in Prakrit (vahassa, vahāya, and vahāi) aside from vahatthaṃ itself.

shrIchihnakAvya

Regarding tādarthye caturthī and the kriyātipatti affixes, here are two examples from Śrīcihnakāvya (a śāstrakāvya dedicated to Prakrit grammar):

sāvā bhīassa siṃhāsaṇam abhaʸapaʸo Uggaseṇāya raṇṇe pacchā kappehisi ttaṃ mahiahiaṇivaṃ pakkhaāṇaṃ vahāi। joggo taṃ ṇo khu hohi ccia ‘hivaïvae bhiccaṭhāṇe mae re ciṭṭhante devarāʸo vi tuha vaʸajue sāhum itthaṃ vaʸanto॥

[śāpād bhītāya siṃhāsanam abhayaprada Ugrasenāya rājñe paścād kalpayiṣyasi tvaṃ mathitvādhikanṛpaṃ pakṣagānāṃ vadhāya। yogyas tvaṃ naḥ khalu bhavaivādhipatipade bhṛtyasthāne mayi re tiṣṭhati devarājo ‘pi tava padayuge sādhum ittham vadan॥]

Having crushed the foremost king (Kaṃsa) to destroy his partisans (Jarāsandha, et al.), thou, the bestower of fearlessness, shalt then prepare the throne for King Ugrasena, who is fearful due to the curse (of Yayāti), and address him fittingly thus:

“Thou art worthy, so assume the position of our overlord. So long as I stand in the station of thy servant, even the King of the Gods (i.e. Śakra) lies at thy two feet!”

In the above verse, we can see the substitution of the dative by the genitive (“bhīassa”), the retention of Skt. tādarthye “-Ṅe” (“Uggaseṇāya raṇṇe”), and an example of the application of the affix “-Ḍāi” to “vaha-.”

homāṇa dukkhaṃ taʸa dhammaloö hontāsuhaṃ hojja bhaʸaṃ ca hojjā। Kaṃso ṭhio e pahu suṭṭhu jāaṃ vippā vaehiṃti tumaṃ hari tti॥

[abhaviṣyad duhkhaṃ tathā dharmalopo ‘bhaviṣyad aśubhaṃ abhaviṣyad bhayaṃ cābhaviṣyat। Kaṃsaḥ sthitaś cet prabho suṣṭhu jātam viprā vakṣyanti tvām haraʸ iti॥]

“O Lord! Had Kaṃsa still been standing (i.e. living), there would have been sorrow, neglect of dharma, inauspiciousness, and fear. It has turned out well (i.e. it is wonderful that Kaṃsa was slain)!” In this way, the vipras (learned Brāhmaṇas) shall address thee, [saying] “O Hari!”

Here we see the use of homāṇa, honta, hojja, hojjā all used in the sense “abhaviṣyat,” corresponding to my earlier statement that finite verbs which express kriyātipatti or conditional mood can take the affixes -jja, -jjā, -nta, or -māṇa.

āśīr-liṅ, vidhi-liṅ

In literary Prakrit, the role of the morphological Sanskrit precative/benedictive (āśīr-liṅ) is subsumed by the morphological imperative (loṭ) and the morphological Skt. optative/potential (vidhi-liṅ) exists only vestigially.

In other words, the Prakrit “vidhyādi” verbal mood morphologically corresponds to Sanskrit loṭ, yet semantically covers the functions of āśīr-liṅ and vidhi-liṅ as well.

The endings for “vidhyādi” mood are -(d)u, -su/-hi, and -mu in the singular and -ntu, -(d)ha, and -mo in the plural.

For example:
Pkt. hasaü/hasadu covers the functions of Skt. hasatu, hasatāt, haset, and hasyāt
Pkt. hasasu/hasahi covers the functions of Skt. hasa, hasatāt, haseḥ, and hasyāḥ
Pkt. hasamu covers the functions of Skt. hasāni, haseyam, and hasyāsam Pkt. hasantu/hasentu covers the functions of Skt. pl. hasantu, haseyuḥ, and hasyāsuḥ as well as Skt. du. hasatām, hasetām, and hasyāstām
Pkt. hasaha/hasadha covers the functions of Skt. pl. hasata, haseta and hasyāsta as well as Skt. du. hasatam, hasetam, and hasyāstam
Pkt. hasāmo covers the functions of Skt. pl. hasāma, hasema, and hasyāsma as well as Skt. du. hasāva, haseva, and hasyāsva
Pkt. suṇaü/suṇeü/suṇāü/suṇadu/suṇedu/suṇādu covers the functions of Skt. śṛṇotu, śṛṇutāt, śṛṇuyāt, and śrūyāt
Pkt. gaṇhasu/geṇhasu/giṇhasu/gaṇhahi/geṇhahi/giṇhahi covers the functions of Skt. gṛhāṇa, gṛhṇītāt, gṛhṇīyāḥ, and gṛhyāḥ
Pkt. haṇasu/haṇahi/hammasu/hammahi covers the functions of Skt. jahi, hatāt, hanyāḥ, and vadhyāḥ
etc.

-jja or -jjā

The endings for the “vidhyādi” verbal mood as well as the vartamānā (present tense) and bhaviṣyantī (simple future tense) endings are optionally replaced by the extremely versatile endings -jja or -jjā in all three persons for both singular and plural (cf. vartamānā-bhaviṣyantyoś ca jja jjā vā).

Finite verbs expressing kriyātipatti or conditional mood can take the affixes -jja, -jjā, -nta, or -māṇa.

However, unlike śatranta/śānajantas which end in the affixes -nta or -māṇa, these kriyātipatti verbs ending in the affixes “-nta” and “-māṇa” don’t decline for person.

With regard to the second/middle person singular ending “-su/-hi,” Prakrit dhātus ending in “-a” may also optionally take the endings ijjasu, ijjahi, jje or luk elision (-∅) in place of the ending -su.

So for Skt. hasa, hasatāt, haseḥ, and hasyāḥ the possible Prakrit forms are hasasu/hasahi/hasijja/hasijjā/hasijjasu/hasijjahi/hasijje/hasa

The MIA/Prakrit suffixal element “-jja/-jjā” diachronically corresponds to the OIA/Sanskrit yāsŨṬ-āgama (e.g. Skt. kuryāt > kujjā).

It is therefore regarded as a vestigial remnant of the morphological Sanskrit optative/potential.

Likewise, the Pkt. vidhyādi affix -su likely descends from the ātmanepada loṭ affix -sva (cf. thāsas se, savābhyāṃ vāmau) while -hi clearly descends from parasmaipada loṭ affix -hi (cf. ser hy apic ca).

voicing to elision

Several Sanskrit intervocalic stops (i.e. k, g, c, j, t, d, p) as well as two semi-vowels (i.e. y and v) generally undergo voicing (if applicable), spirantization/fricativization (if applicable), and elision (cf. ka-ga-ca-ja-ta-da-pa-ya-vāṃ prāyo luk) in MIA.

For example, the “t” in OIA/Sanskrit gata- (“gone”) undergoes voicing to gada-, spirantization to gaða- and finally elision to ga∅a.

This word gaä- can optionally take ya-śruti or epenthesis with a “y”-glide in order to resolve the hiatus, resulting in the form gaya-.

Likewise, the “k” in bālaka- is voiced to bālaga-, spirantized to bālaɣa- and ultimately elided to give bālaʸa-.

Literary Śāurasenī and Māgadhī generally retain the voiced stops (gada-, bālaga-, etc.) whereas Mahārāṣṭrī generally uses forms with elided stops (gaʸa-, bālaʸa-, etc.)

The intervocalic aspirated stops kh, gh, th, dh, and bh typically become “h” in Mahārāṣṭrī (kha-gha-tha-dha-bhāṃ [haḥ]), e.g. sakhi- becomes sahi-, Raghupati- becomes Rahuvaï-, surabhi- becomes surahi- etc.

The intervocalic retroflex stop -ṭ- is generally voiced to -ḍ-, e.g. lalāṭa- becomes ṇalāḍa-/ṇilāḍa- (also ṇaḍāla-/ṇiḍāla-, via vyatyaya/metathesis).

The ṭ in the Skt. words saṭā, śakaṭa, and kaiṭabha takes substitution with aspirated ḍh, resulting in the forms saḍhā, saʸaḍha and keḍhava respectively.

Doubled consonant

One of the most ubiquitous features of Prakrit is the assimilation of one consonant into another, ultimately resulting in a geminate/doubled consonant. Hemacandra lists this gemination rule as “anādau śeṣādeśayor dvitvam.” In order for this gemination to occur, the consonant cluster cannot occur at the start of a word. As an example, the cluster “kṣ” in the word yakṣa- (“tree spirit/guardian”) is replaced by “kh” due to kṣaḥ khaḥ kvacit tu cha-jhau. This “kṣ” is not word-initial, so the kh which takes its place will undergo doubling/gemination to khkh. The letter “kh” is the second member of the kavarga, so the first “kh” in the cluster “khkh” will undergo substitution by unaspirated “k” (cf. dvitīya-turyayor upari pūrvaḥ), resulting in the cluster “kkh.” The “y” of yakṣa- is word initial and therefore takes substitution in “j” (cf. āder yo jaḥ), resulting in the final Prakrit form jakkha-. The “sph” of Skt. sphoṭaka- also takes substitution in “kha” (cf. kṣveṭakādau), yet this “kh” will not undergo gemination because the “sph” cluster is word initial. Consequently, the resultant (Mahārāṣṭrī) Prakrit form is khoḍaʸa, not *kkhoḍaʸa.

Perfect tense gone

The morphological perfect of Sanskrit/OIA is essentially extinct in MIA unless one includes forms such as āha and vidu.
The morphological imperfect survives only in a handful of fossilized forms (e.g. Pkt. abbavī for Skt. abravīt).

The morphological aorist survives to a somewhat greater extent than the perfect and imperfect, though its conjugational system has been drastically simplified.

The endings -sī, -hī, and -hīa are prescribed for expressing past time (bhūtārtha) without preserving distinctions between the three tenses.

Pkt. kāsī/kāhī/kāhīa may morphologically correspond to Skt. akārṣīt but they can also be used to express the sense of Skt. akarot and cakāra. Likewise, Pkt. ṭhāsī/ṭhāhī/ṭhāhīa correspond not only to Skt. asthāt but also to atiṣṭhat and tasthau. Furthermore, consonant-final verbal stems may take the ending -īa (cf. vyañjanād īaḥ), so Pkt. huvīa covers Skt. abhūt, abhavat, and babhūva, Pkt. acchīa covers Skt. āsiṣṭa, āsta, and āsāñcakre and Pkt. geṇhīa covers Skt. agrahīt, agṛhṇāt, and jagrāha. However, the most common way to express the past in Prakrit is to bypass the finite past-tense verb forms altogether and use the past passive participles (ktāntas) as the predicate.

In the case of intransitive verbs, the past participle generally agrees with the agent, e.g.

saṃpatto Ujjeṇiṃ tubbhehiṃ samaṃ ca milio ham (samprāpta Ujjayinīṃ yuṣmābhiḥ samaṃ ca milito ‘ham)

I arrived in Ujjayinī and met with you (pl.).

With transitive verbs, the past participle generally agrees with the patient with the agent typically taking the instrumental case, e.g.

Sīā-paütti-heuṃ Pavaṇasuo Rāhaveṇa āṇatto
(Sītā-pravṛtti-hetuṃ Pavana-suto Rāghaveṇājñāptaḥ)
Rāma instructed Hanumān to obtain news regarding Sītā.
- Dhūrtākhyāna (Haribhadra)
